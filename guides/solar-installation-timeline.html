Solar Installation Timeline: Permits, Inspections and Permission to Operate

The panels can be on your roof and the job can still be unfinished. A local permit, a utility interconnection review, a passed inspection and Permission to Operate are different milestones. If your installer says the project is “waiting on the utility,” ask what was submitted, when it was submitted and what the utility still needs. That is a much better update than another vague promise that PTO is coming soon.

First, the rule that matters: Do not switch on a grid-connected solar system just because installation is complete or the local inspection passed. Follow the serving utility's interconnection requirements and wait for the authorization it requires. Utilities can impose different steps, and some require pre-installation approval as well as final PTO.

A residential solar project usually involves at least three parties: the installer, the local permitting authority and the electric utility. They do not approve the same thing. The local authority checks the installation against applicable building and electrical requirements. The utility checks the proposed connection to its grid and decides when the system may operate in parallel with it.

The National Renewable Energy Laboratory's review of SolarAPP+ describes this as a process involving design, permit preparation, local review, installation, inspection and utility permission to operate. Some utilities also require interconnection approval before construction. The steps can overlap, but one approval does not replace the other.

How long does a home solar installation take?

There is no dependable nationwide number. NLR analyzed more than 200,000 solar projects and found that adoption times vary widely. Local permitting practices, utility review, the installer's workload and project-specific electrical or grid work all affect the schedule. Its research also finds that faster permitting practices can shorten the process, which is another reason not to treat one installer's estimate as a national standard.

Utility examples make the variation concrete. Oncor says installation timing can range from a few days to several months. PSE&G publishes different inspection processing windows for different application levels after it receives the required construction-approval documents. Those are utility-specific procedures, not a promise for another service territory.

Ask your installer for a milestone schedule with the responsible party and the submission date for each application. “Six weeks” is not a useful project plan if nobody can tell you whether the permit has been submitted or the utility has accepted the interconnection package.

What happens, and in what order?

The outline below is common, not universal. Your utility may require an interconnection review before installation, while another utility may complete important steps after the local inspection. Some work can run in parallel.

1. Final design and application package

After the site assessment, the installer prepares the final electrical design and equipment schedule. The permit package may include a site plan, panel layout, electrical diagrams, equipment specifications and supporting calculations. The utility application uses its own forms and technical details. The homeowner may need to sign a tariff application, authorization or interconnection agreement.

Before the installer submits anything, make sure the equipment and system size in the paperwork match the proposal you signed. A later change to panel count, inverter, service equipment or system capacity can mean revised drawings or another review.

2. Local permit review and utility interconnection review

Your city, county or other Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) reviews the permit application. The AHJ may be a building department, electrical inspector or another local office responsible for enforcing the applicable codes. It can request corrected drawings or additional information before issuing a permit.

The utility separately reviews the proposed connection. Depending on its rules and the system, it may check equipment certification, system size, service capacity, protection settings or whether a grid study or upgrade is needed. Some utilities issue an approval to build before the panels go up. Do not assume the installer can start construction just because the city issued a permit; ask whether the utility has a pre-installation approval requirement.

3. Installation and any required electrical work

Once the approvals required before construction are in place, the installer schedules the crew. The work can include mounting the modules, installing inverters and disconnects, running conductors, completing grounding and labeling, and making any approved service-panel changes.

If the electrician finds that the existing service equipment needs an upgrade, that can add a separate permit, utility coordination and inspection. A panel upgrade is not a minor scheduling detail. Ask whether it is included in the contract, who is responsible for the utility work and whether the solar schedule depends on it.

4. Local inspection and corrections

After installation, the AHJ may inspect the electrical work and, where required, the building or roof-related work. The inspector checks compliance with the permit and applicable codes. If something is incomplete or incorrect, the installer must address the correction and arrange whatever follow-up inspection the authority requires.

Get the actual inspection result. “Inspection is done” could mean it was requested, scheduled, partially completed or passed. Those are four different statuses.

5. Final documents and utility PTO review

Once the required local approvals are complete, the installer submits the closeout package the utility asks for. Depending on the utility, that may include the signed inspection approval, certificate of completion, as-built drawings, equipment details, photographs, test records or a completed interconnection agreement.

The utility may review the documents, request corrections, arrange a meter change or conduct its own inspection. Not every utility sends someone to the property. PG&E says most customers do not need an on-site utility inspection before PTO, although some applications are selected for inspection and customers without a smart meter may need a meter-related visit. Your utility's process controls.

6. Written Permission to Operate and activation

PTO is the utility's authorization for the system to operate under its interconnection requirements. The notice may arrive by email, letter or an online customer/installer portal. Save it with the permit and inspection records.

After authorization, the installer or another authorized party can complete the activation steps required by the utility and equipment manufacturer. Ask for a handoff showing that the system is operating, monitoring is set up and any export meter or billing arrangement is in place.

What commonly holds up a solar project?

Most delays fall into a handful of buckets. The useful question is not simply how long the project has been waiting. It is which bucket it is in and what event moves it forward.

  • Incomplete permit drawings: the AHJ asks for a revised site plan, electrical diagram, equipment detail or calculation. Ask for the correction notice and the date the revised package went back in.
  • Utility application errors: the system size, inverter model, service details or customer signature does not match what the utility needs. Ask whether the application is accepted as complete or still marked deficient.
  • Main-panel or service upgrades: the home needs electrical work or utility coordination before the solar system can proceed. Get the scope, responsible contractor and inspection sequence in writing.
  • Inspection corrections: labels, disconnects, wiring, grounding or other installation details need attention. Ask for the written correction list, not a second-hand summary.
  • Meter work: the utility needs to install, replace or reprogram a meter. Confirm whether that is a condition for PTO or a follow-on task after PTO in your utility's process.
  • Grid study or network upgrade: the utility identifies a transformer, service or distribution-system issue. Ask for the study result, the required work, who pays under the applicable tariff and whether the utility has provided a schedule. Do not accept an unsupported date from the installer as a utility commitment.
  • Installer backlog or poor handoff: the city or utility may be ready, but the installer has not submitted the next document, booked the correction or scheduled commissioning. Ask for the actual submission receipt or appointment confirmation.
Pro Tip Here is the real-world catch: a system can be physically complete while the file is still sitting in a city inbox, an installer queue or a utility review. Those are different bottlenecks. Ask for the submission receipt and the exact outstanding item before accepting another estimated completion date. My take: “We're waiting on PTO” is not a status report. It should come with an application number, the date the complete package was submitted, the utility's current status and the next action owner. If nobody can provide those four things, you do not yet know where the delay sits.

PTO is taking longer than expected. What should you ask?

Start with the installer, because it normally has the permit and interconnection paperwork. Ask for a written status update that answers these questions:

  1. What date did the local authority pass the final inspection? Can you send me the approval or certificate?
  2. What date did you submit the final interconnection/PTO package to the utility?
  3. What is the utility application or notification number?
  4. Does the utility show the application as received, under review, incomplete, approved or waiting on field work?
  5. Has the utility requested a correction, missing document, meter change, study or grid upgrade? Please forward the request.
  6. Is there any action still open with the installer, electrician, AHJ or homeowner?
  7. Who owns the next action, and when will you update me if the status has not changed?

If the installer says the utility has the file, contact the utility's distributed-generation or interconnection team using the official number or portal. Give them the application number and ask what remains outstanding. Do not send account credentials or sensitive information to an unverified email address.

If the installer and utility give different answers, ask each to identify the missing document or unresolved milestone. A date-stamped submission receipt is more useful than a sales representative's estimate. Keep the answers, notices and inspection records together.

You do not need to become the project manager, but you should not have to rely on somebody else's memory when a delay or billing dispute comes up.

  • [ ] Utility interconnection application/notification number and submission confirmation.

  • [ ] Meter work confirmation, if applicable, and the utility's explanation of when it occurs.
  • [ ] Commissioning record, monitoring access, operating instructions and warranty documents.

Common questions about solar permits and PTO

Can solar panels be installed before the utility approves the interconnection?

It depends on the utility's process. Some utilities require approval to build before installation; others allow installation while parts of the interconnection review continue. NREL's process review explicitly notes that certain utilities require pre-installation interconnection approval. Check your utility's current rules before assuming the installer can proceed.

Does a passed city inspection mean I can turn the system on?

No. The local inspection is one part of the project. The utility may still need final documents, an interconnection agreement, a meter step or another review. Follow the utility's written authorization requirements.

Does the utility always inspect the solar system in person?

No. Requirements vary. PG&E says most customers do not need an on-site utility inspection before PTO, although some applications are selected for inspection and some meter situations require a visit. Your local AHJ may still require its own inspection.

Can the system operate before PTO if the installer limits export?

Do not assume that. Some utilities and approved system configurations may have specific non-export or testing procedures, but they are not a blanket exception. Ask the utility and follow the written interconnection agreement and activation instructions. Do not energize the system based on advice from a forum post or an informal installer message.

Does PTO mean my utility will immediately credit exported electricity?

Not necessarily. Meter programming, billing enrollment and the retail electricity provider's export-credit plan can be separate steps. Oncor, for example, says its meter reprogramming follows PTO and that surplus-generation data is provided to the retail electric provider on a later schedule. Confirm the exact meter and billing status with your utility and electricity supplier.

Who should I contact if the installer stops responding?

Use the utility application number to check the interconnection status directly with the utility. For permit status, contact the local AHJ. Keep written records of open contract obligations and payments. If the installer has stopped operating, the utility can explain the application status, but it may not take over the installer's contractual or commissioning responsibilities.

Primary sources and utility examples

  • National Laboratory of the Rockies: Solar Permitting, Inspection, and Interconnection Timelines — analysis of more than 200,000 U.S. solar projects and the roles of installers, local authorities and utilities.
  • National Renewable Energy Laboratory: SolarAPP+ Performance Review — describes the typical residential permitting, inspection and interconnection sequence, including utilities that require approval before installation.
  • U.S. Department of Energy: Permitting and Inspection for Rooftop Solar — explains local permitting and inspection and why requirements differ by jurisdiction.
  • Oncor: Solar and Interconnection FAQs — its application, interconnection agreement, PTO and meter sequence.
  • PSE&G: Building and Connecting Your Home Solar System — required closeout documents, utility inspection process and explicit instruction not to turn on the system before PTO.
  • PG&E: Contractor Resources for Interconnection — explains when an on-site utility inspection may or may not be required and meter-related exceptions.

Utility procedures and published processing windows can change. The examples above illustrate differences between service territories; they are not a substitute for the current tariff, interconnection agreement or instructions from the utility serving your address.

Reader note: This guide explains the common residential grid-connected process. It does not determine the permit, inspection, interconnection, metering or operating requirements for a particular property. Confirm those requirements with the local AHJ and serving utility.
