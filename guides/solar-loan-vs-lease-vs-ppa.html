Solar Loan vs. Lease vs. PPA in 2026: Which Contract Are You Actually Signing?

Two solar offers can show nearly the same monthly payment and leave you with completely different obligations. One may be a loan and you own the equipment. Another may be a 25-year lease or PPA with transfer rules when you sell the house. Stop comparing the payment first. Find out who owns the system, what you owe over the full term and what happens when you move.

Start here: Cash purchase and most solar loans leave the homeowner owning the system, subject to the loan agreement and any security interest. A lease or PPA leaves ownership with a third-party provider. A lease usually charges a set payment, sometimes with an annual increase. A PPA charges for solar electricity under the contract's per-kWh terms. None of these arrangements guarantees a zero utility bill.

Solar-loan fees: compare the cash price with the actual amount financed

A low APR is not proof of a cheap loan. The amount financed may be higher than the cash price because of dealer or program fees, and the payment may assume a lump-sum prepayment later. Get the cash price for the exact same equipment and installation scope. Then compare it with the loan principal, APR and total scheduled payments.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's August 7, 2024 solar-financing report documented loan structures where lender or program fees pushed the principal above the system's cash price. In the material it reviewed, fees were commonly 10%–30% of cash price, with some above 50%. That is a historical finding from the CFPB's review, not a claim about every loan being sold today. Use it as a reason to inspect the fee disclosure, not as a shortcut for accusing a lender.

CFPB illustration: a $30,000 cash project plus $9,000 added to the loan produces $39,000 in principal before interest. That $9,000 gap is 30% of the cash price. In a real quote, the gap could be a dealer fee, a battery, different equipment or extra work. Make the installer and lender itemize it before calling it a fee. If they cannot explain the difference, do not sign.
Pro Tip Here is the real-world catch: the “monthly payment” on the sales sheet may be based on a loan that expects you to make a large extra payment later. If you do not make it, the payment can rise or the term can change under the contract. Ask for the schedule both with and without the assumed prepayment. Then check whether the sales pitch uses a federal homeowner tax credit that is no longer available for new qualifying expenditures after December 31, 2025.

2026 homeowner tax-credit assumption: do not compare a real loan with an assumed “net price”

The IRS states that the homeowner Residential Clean Energy Credit under Section 25D is not available for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. For an individual item, the IRS says the expenditure is treated as made when the original installation is completed; paying before the deadline does not preserve the credit if installation is completed after it. If a 2026 sales presentation subtracts an assumed 30% homeowner credit from the project price or expects you to use that credit for a loan prepayment, ask the seller to show the full contract price, actual loan principal and scheduled payments without that assumed credit. Check the IRS Section 25D timing FAQs for the current rule and seek qualified tax advice for individual circumstances. This is the federal homeowner Section 25D credit only; state, local and utility programs are separate, and third-party-owned arrangements can have different tax treatment.

Get these items before signing: cash price, amount financed, APR, term, every dealer/program/origination fee, any cash paid separately, monthly payment schedule, total of payments and prepayment rules. Ask whether the loan payment changes if you do not make a tax-credit prepayment. If the sales sheet only shows a “net system cost,” demand the actual principal and legal payment schedule.

What solar customers argue about: escalators and the long contract tail

r/solar discussions about leases and PPAs are often sharply divided. One 2025 homeowner weighing a 25-year PPA with a 2.99% annual escalator focused on whether the payment would stay below the utility bill; replies pushed back on the long-term cost and the risk of assuming future utility rates would rise fast enough. That thread is one contract discussion, not proof that every PPA is bad. Read the PPA discussion.

My take: a lease or PPA is not automatically a scam, and ownership is not automatically a good deal. But an escalator means the payment rises by contract whether your utility raises its rates or not. Calculate every year's payment, add the remaining utility bill, check production guarantees and read the home-sale transfer rules. If the salesperson only shows year-one savings, the comparison is unfinished.
