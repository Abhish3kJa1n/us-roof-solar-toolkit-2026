Solar Loan vs. Lease vs. PPA in 2026: Which Contract Are You Actually Signing?

Solar proposals can look similar while transferring very different costs, ownership rights and home-sale obligations. Compare the cash price, amount financed, interest, escalators, system ownership, maintenance and contract transfer terms—not just the advertised monthly payment.

Home / Guides / Solar Loan vs Lease vs PPA
First distinction: With a cash purchase or solar loan, the homeowner generally owns the system after purchase. With a lease or power-purchase agreement (PPA), a third party owns the system and the homeowner pays under a long-term contract. A lease commonly charges a fixed periodic amount; a PPA charges for contract-defined solar electricity produced or supplied. In every case, you may still have a utility bill.

The FTC explains that with a lease or PPA the homeowner does not own the system; the contract determines payment increases, maintenance, end-of-term options, transfer requirements and who receives any applicable incentives. The exact agreement controls. Do not assume a salesperson's verbal description overrides the contract.

Solar-loan fees: compare the cash price with the actual amount financed

A low advertised APR or monthly payment does not, by itself, tell you the total cost of a solar loan. Ask for a written cash purchase price for the same equipment and work scope, then compare it with the loan principal and all payments.

In an August 2024 Issue Spotlight, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) documented solar-specific loan structures in which lender or program fees increased the loan principal above the system's cash price. The CFPB reported fees commonly in the 10%–30% range of the cash price in the material it reviewed, with some exceeding 50%. Its report described lender practices and consumer complaints at that time; it is not a current market-wide prevalence estimate and does not mean every solar loan includes such fees.

Illustration based on the CFPB's example: a $30,000 cash proposal plus $9,000 in added loan principal produces a $39,000 loan before interest. The $9,000 difference is 30% of the cash price. In an actual proposal, that difference could reflect a dealer/program fee, different equipment, a battery, other work or a mismatch in scope. Do not label it a hidden fee until the lender and installer itemise it.

Request all of the following in writing: cash price, loan principal, APR, loan term, origination/program/dealer charges, any fees paid outside the loan, payment schedule, total of payments, prepayment rules and any balloon or re-amortization provision. If the seller only shows a “net system cost” that assumes a tax credit or future payment, ask to see the full legal amount owed and the full payment schedule.
