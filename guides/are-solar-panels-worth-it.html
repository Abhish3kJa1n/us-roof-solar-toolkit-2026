Are Solar Panels Worth It? Calculate the Real Payback

Solar can reduce a household's electricity costs, but the financial result depends on the price you pay, how much power you use while the system is producing, what the utility pays for exports, financing terms and the condition of your roof. A national average cannot answer those questions for your home.

Home / Guides / Are Solar Panels Worth It?
Quick answer: Solar is more likely to make financial sense when the roof is suitable, the system is priced competitively, the household has meaningful electricity use during solar hours or fair export compensation, and the expected savings justify the full cost over the period you expect to own the home. It may be a poor deal when the roof needs major work, the quote is inflated, export credits are low, financing charges are heavy or the system is oversized. There is no reliable yes/no answer without the actual tariff and proposal.

The five numbers that decide whether solar is worth it

Before comparing panels or installer brands, collect five pieces of evidence. These matter more to the financial result than a sales presentation showing a large annual production number.

  1. Annual electricity use: Use 12 months of utility bills and record kWh, not only the total dollar amount. Bills can include fixed charges, taxes, demand charges and other amounts that solar may not remove.
  2. Solar production estimate: Get a location- and roof-specific estimate that states system size, orientation, tilt, shade, system losses and estimated annual kWh. Do not accept an unsupported generic production figure.
  3. Value of self-consumed power: Identify the marginal energy charge avoided when the home uses one less kWh from the utility. This is not necessarily the bill's average all-in price.
  4. Export compensation: Confirm the exact tariff credit for electricity sent to the grid, including whether it varies by time, month, system size or enrollment date.
  5. All-in installed price: Compare the cash price including equipment, labor, electrical work, permits, utility interconnection, roof work, battery, dealer fees and any other required items. Separate optional upgrades from required work.

The U.S. Department of Energy says household savings depend on electricity consumption, system size and production, roof direction and sunlight, electricity rates and the utility's compensation for excess generation. It also notes that the amount of savings is highly household-specific; a national average cannot determine an individual home's payback.

How to calculate the value of the electricity a system produces

Solar production is not automatically worth the full retail price of electricity. A home may consume some generation immediately and export the rest. Each flow can have a different value under the utility tariff.

Cash simple paybackAll-in cash price ÷ first-year modeled annual net energy valueComplete quote and defensible annual value estimate

This is a first-pass framework, not a complete lifetime investment model. A simple payback calculation does not discount future cash flows and can omit panel degradation, inverter replacement, maintenance, insurance, financing interest, future utility-rate changes, taxes, opportunity cost and the time value of money. A financed system also needs a cash-flow comparison that includes loan payments, fees and escalators—not just the cash price divided by modeled energy value.

For a more accurate analysis, ask the installer for monthly production, monthly imports and exports, estimated bills under the actual tariff and a comparison against your current utility bill. If the utility uses time-of-use pricing, tiered rates or demand charges, an annual one-rate model is not enough.

The utility tariff can change the result substantially

Two homes with similar solar systems can receive different financial value if their utilities credit exported electricity differently. Some tariffs value exports close to the retail energy rate; others use a lower avoided-cost or time-dependent credit. Customer charges, minimum bills, demand charges, time-of-use periods and interconnection rules also matter.

Do not assume that one exported kWh cancels one imported kWh at the same price. The U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner guidance specifically says export compensation depends on state policy and the local electric utility, and recommends checking eligible customer types, system-size limits, rates and bill-credit design.

The EIA's state residential electricity averages are useful for broad market context, but they are not your utility's marginal import price and are not an export-credit schedule. Use your actual tariff and utility bill. Our state electricity price reference is a comparison tool, not a tariff lookup.

Roof condition can turn a good solar quote into a bad project sequence

Solar panels can remain in service for decades, so installing them over a roof that needs replacement soon can create a second project. Before signing, establish the roof's condition, age, material, remaining useful life and whether repairs or replacement are likely during the solar system's expected service period.

If roof replacement is likely, compare the total cost and disruption of completing roofing before solar with the written costs and responsibilities for later panel removal, storage, reinstallation, electrical work and warranty handling. Do not assume the solar installer will pay for future detach-and-reset work or that the roofing contractor will handle the electrical side.

Roof orientation, shading, usable roof area, setbacks, penetrations, structural capacity and local fire/building rules also affect suitability. The DOE advises that some rooftops may not be suitable because of age or shade, and that roof condition should be part of the project assessment.

Read what to check before installing solar on an existing roof. If the roof itself is in question, use the repair-versus-replacement decision guide before treating the solar proposal as a standalone investment.

Compare the full cash price and the actual financing contract

For a current installed-price reference and a PV-only quote-per-watt calculation, see the solar installation cost guide.

Request a cash price even when the salesperson leads with a monthly payment. A financed proposal can include interest, dealer fees, origination costs, escalators, prepaid amounts or contract conditions that are difficult to see in a payment-only presentation. Compare equivalent system sizes, equipment, warranties, battery scope, electrical upgrades and roof work.

For a loan, ask for the amount financed, APR, term, total scheduled payments, fees, prepayment terms, collateral or lien terms and whether the quote assumes a future tax payment or other lump-sum prepayment. If a payment is expected to rise after a promotional period, include that in the comparison.

For a lease or power-purchase agreement (PPA), the homeowner is not necessarily buying the system. Compare the monthly payment or per-kWh price, annual escalator, term, production guarantee, maintenance obligations, roof work, transfer/sale process, buyout terms and what happens if the provider stops operating. A lease/PPA does not have the same homeowner payback calculation as a cash purchase.

Get multiple proposals and compare the same system scope. A lower price is not a bargain if it omits electrical upgrades, roof repairs, required equipment, warranties or utility-interconnection work.

2026 federal homeowner solar tax-credit status

Important current-law check: As of November 1, 2026, the IRS states that the Residential Clean Energy Credit under Section 25D is not allowed for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. For an individual item, the IRS says the expenditure is generally treated as made when original installation is completed. Paying for a system before the deadline does not preserve the credit if installation is completed after December 31, 2025. Do not subtract a 30% Section 25D homeowner credit from a new 2026 purchase quote unless current IRS guidance establishes eligibility for the specific expenditure.

State, local and utility incentives are separate and may have different eligibility, funding and deadlines. Verify each program with the responsible government agency or utility before including it in the economics. Do not rely on a solar salesperson's verbal incentive claim or an old online calculator. Tax treatment is individual; consult a qualified tax professional.

Third-party-owned lease and PPA arrangements are different from a homeowner-owned system. Do not assume a provider's tax treatment automatically becomes a homeowner tax credit or a guaranteed price reduction. Evaluate the actual contract price and payment schedule.

When solar may not be worth the cost

  • The roof is heavily shaded, structurally unsuitable or likely to need major replacement before the system's economics recover its cost.
  • The installed price is high relative to the expected usable generation and the value of that energy.
  • The utility pays little for exports and household demand is concentrated at times when the array is not producing.
  • The proposal is oversized relative to the home's consumption and applicable export rules.
  • Financing charges, dealer fees or annual payment escalators consume too much of the expected savings.
  • The homeowner expects to move before the economics make sense and has not established how the system or contract will transfer or be valued.
  • The sales estimate depends on a tax credit, rebate, utility rate increase or export rate that has not been verified.

These are screening questions, not automatic disqualifiers. A homeowner may value resilience, emissions reduction or energy-price stability in addition to direct financial payback. Keep those non-financial benefits separate from the cash-return calculation so the decision is transparent.

Pre-contract solar economics checklist

  1. Gather 12 months of utility bills and identify the current tariff and marginal energy charge.
  2. Obtain an annual production estimate with system size, orientation, tilt, shading, losses and expected variability stated.
  3. Ask for modeled monthly self-consumption, grid imports and exports—not only annual offset.
  4. Get the exact utility export-credit rate and confirm interconnection eligibility directly with the utility.
  5. Obtain a complete cash quote and compare equipment, labor, permitting, electrical work, battery and roof-related scope.
  6. Review loan, lease or PPA documents independently; compare total obligations, not just the advertised monthly payment.
  7. Confirm roof condition and expected remaining service life before installation.
  8. Verify current federal, state, local and utility incentives using official sources.
  9. Keep the production model, tariff, quote, financing schedule and assumptions used in your calculation.

Common questions

Are solar panels worth it without the 30% federal homeowner tax credit?

They can be, but the project must work using its actual net price and utility value without assuming a Section 25D credit for a new 2026 expenditure. Compare the cash price, avoided import charges, export compensation, roof condition and the period you expect to own the home.

Does a high electric bill mean solar will pay for itself?

Not necessarily. The bill may include fixed charges or demand charges that solar does not remove. You need the annual kWh use, applicable energy rate, expected direct-use share, export credit and full system price.

Is EIA's state electricity average the right rate for my solar calculator?

No. It is a broad average for residential customers in a state. Your marginal import price and export credit come from your utility tariff and can differ by time period, plan and customer class.

Does a PVWatts estimate guarantee the system's production?

No. PVWatts is a simplified model that uses assumptions and weather data. The provider says predictions include inherent assumptions and uncertainties and do not represent every site-specific characteristic. Ask an installer for a detailed site-specific design and production estimate.

Should I replace the roof before installing solar?

There is no universal roof-age cutoff. Obtain a qualified roof-condition assessment and compare the cost and contract responsibilities of doing roof work before solar with any later panel removal and reinstallation.

Does a lease or PPA give me the homeowner tax credit?

Do not assume so. A lease or PPA is a third-party ownership/contract arrangement, not the same as buying a system under Section 25D. Review the contract and verify any claimed discount or incentive with official sources and a qualified tax professional.
