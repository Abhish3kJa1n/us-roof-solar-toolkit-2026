Are Solar Panels Worth It? Calculate the Real Payback

Solar can cut a power bill. It can also leave a homeowner paying a loan on a system that saves far less than the sales pitch promised. The difference usually comes down to five things: the installed price, when the house uses electricity, what the utility pays for exported power, the financing contract and whether the roof is ready for panels. State averages and glossy production charts won't settle the deal. Your utility tariff and the actual contract will.

The straight answer: Solar is worth serious consideration when the roof has enough useful life left, the system price is defensible, the utility gives your exported power a reasonable value and the savings hold up after every fee and financing cost. Walk away from the sales math if it relies on a tax credit you cannot claim, retail-rate credits your utility does not offer, or a payment that only works if you make a future lump-sum payment. Get the tariff and full contract before believing the payback figure.
Pro Tip: Here is the real-world catch: A solar proposal can look cheap because the price is buried in a loan with a dealer fee, while a roofing quote can look cheap because it excludes rotten decking, flashing, extra tear-off layers or permit work. Those are different trades, but the homeowner gets hit the same way: the headline number was never the complete job. Make each contractor write down the included scope, exclusions, unit prices for likely extras and who signs off on hidden damage before work starts. A legitimate change order should identify the newly discovered condition, show the price and get your approval before the extra work proceeds.

The five numbers that decide whether solar is worth it

Forget the panel-brand debate for a minute. A fancy module cannot rescue a bad tariff or an overpriced contract. Get these five numbers in writing first.

  1. Your real annual usage: Pull 12 months of bills and total the kWh. The dollar total is not enough. Fixed customer charges, taxes and demand charges may remain even after solar is installed.
  2. A credible production estimate: The proposal should show system size, roof planes, azimuth, tilt, shade, losses and monthly output. If the salesperson gives you one big annual kWh number but cannot show how it was built, treat it as a claim, not evidence.
  3. What each self-used kWh saves: Find the marginal energy charge you avoid when the panels power the house. Do not multiply production by the average bill amount. That shortcut can make the savings look better than they are.
  4. What the utility pays for exports: Get the current export rate and the rules that apply to your account. Credits can change by time of day, month, system size and enrollment date.
  5. The complete cash price: Include panels, inverter, mounting, labor, permits, electrical upgrades, interconnection, battery, roof repairs and any required extras. Make the installer separate required work from optional upgrades.

That is why a national “average solar savings” number is close to useless for deciding whether your own quote is good. The Department of Energy points homeowners to the same hard variables: how much power the home uses, what the array can produce, roof direction and sunlight, the retail rate and the utility's export compensation. Your bill and tariff beat a national headline every time.

How to calculate the value of the electricity a system produces

A kilowatt-hour from the roof does not have one guaranteed dollar value. Power used in the house can avoid a retail energy charge. Power sent to the grid may earn a smaller credit, a time-based credit or something else entirely. Treating every generated kWh as if it offsets a full-price utility kWh is one of the easiest ways to make a weak proposal look profitable.

This gives you a screening number, not a promise about the next 25 years. Simple payback ignores the time value of money and can leave out panel degradation, inverter replacement, repairs, insurance, financing costs and changes to the utility tariff. It also says nothing about whether the loan payment strains your monthly budget. For a financed system, compare the actual payment schedule and total amount repaid against the bill savings. Dividing the cash price by a modeled first-year figure does not answer that question.

Demand a month-by-month model: solar production, power used on site, grid imports, exports and the resulting utility bill under your actual rate plan. If your utility uses time-of-use periods, tiers or demand charges, a single annual rate hides the very details that can make or break the economics. Ask for the tariff name and version used in the model. Then check it yourself with the utility.

The utility tariff can change the result substantially

Same panels. Same system size. Different utility rules. One homeowner may get a useful bill credit for exports; another may be paid far less than the retail rate. Fixed customer charges, minimum bills, time-of-use windows, demand charges and interconnection limits can change the result too. The utility tariff is not fine print. It is part of the price of the project.

Never let an installer quietly treat one exported kWh as a full-price credit unless your tariff actually says that. Check the utility's current rules for your customer class, system-size limit, export rate and bill-credit treatment. Ask the utility, not the salesperson. The salesperson is paid to sell the project; the utility owns the tariff.

The EIA state average is useful for comparing broad electricity-price patterns. It is not the rate on your bill, and it tells you nothing about what your utility pays for exports. Use your own tariff. Our state electricity price reference is background context, not a substitute for the utility's rate sheet.

Roof condition can turn a good solar quote into a bad project sequence

A roof that is near the end of its life can turn a solar installation into two expensive jobs. Panels may need to come off before the roofer can replace shingles, then go back on after the work. Before signing the solar contract, get the roof inspected and establish what is damaged, what is sound and how much useful life is realistically left. “It looks fine from the driveway” is not a roof assessment.

If replacement is coming, price the roof work before the panels go up. Get the detach-and-reset cost in writing too, including removal, storage, reinstall, electrical checks and who is responsible if a panel or roof component is damaged. Do not assume the roofer handles solar wiring, or that the solar company will cover roof work. Those gaps are where homeowners get stuck between two contractors pointing at each other.

A usable roof is more than empty space on satellite imagery. Shade, roof direction, pitch, vents, chimneys, fire setbacks, structural capacity and local code all affect the layout. A proposal that packs panels onto every available square foot may not be buildable once the site is measured and local requirements are applied.

Read what to check before installing solar on an existing roof. If the roof itself is in question, use the repair-versus-replacement decision guide before treating the solar proposal as a standalone investment.

Compare the full cash price and the actual financing contract

For a current installed-price reference and a PV-only quote-per-watt calculation, see the solar installation cost guide.

Ask for the cash price before discussing the monthly payment. A low payment can be manufactured with a longer loan, a large dealer fee, a promotional rate that costs more upfront or an assumed lump-sum prepayment later. Compare the same system scope: panel count and model, inverter, battery, electrical work, roof work, permits and warranties. If the salesperson will only discuss the payment, you do not yet have a price you can compare.

For a loan, read the amount financed, APR, term, total of payments, dealer/origination fees, prepayment rules and any lien or security interest. Check whether the payment shown assumes you will send a large lump sum later. If the sales pitch says “use your tax credit to pay down the loan,” stop there: the federal homeowner Section 25D credit is no longer available for qualifying expenditures made after December 31, 2025. Do not build a 2026 budget around a credit you cannot claim.

A lease or PPA is not the same thing as owning the equipment. Read the payment or per-kWh rate, annual escalator, contract length, production guarantee, maintenance duties, roof-work rules, transfer process if you sell the house and any buyout formula. Ask what happens if the provider is sold, shuts down or disputes a warranty claim. Do not compare a lease payment with a loan payment as if the contracts give you the same asset and obligations.

Get at least two or three written proposals with matching scope. A suspiciously cheap quote may simply leave out the main panel upgrade, trenching, roof repair, required rapid shutdown equipment, permit fees or utility work. Make each installer list exclusions. Then ask what could trigger a change order after the crew opens the roof or electrical panel.

Federal homeowner solar tax credit: the 2026 rule

Do not put the old 30% credit in a 2026 quote. The IRS says the Section 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit is not allowed for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. For an individual item, the expenditure is generally treated as made when original installation is completed. A deposit or equipment purchase before the deadline does not save the credit if installation finishes after it. If a proposal still subtracts 30% from a new 2026 homeowner-owned system, ask the salesperson to show the exact IRS rule they are relying on. Do not accept “everyone gets it” as an answer.

State, local and utility programs are a separate question. Some have income limits, equipment rules, application windows or limited funding. Confirm the program is open and that your address, utility and system qualify before subtracting anything from the quote. Keep the program page or written utility confirmation. For tax questions, use a qualified tax professional.

A third-party-owned system may have different tax treatment from a system you buy. That does not mean the provider's tax benefit automatically becomes your credit or a guaranteed discount. Judge the offer by the contract price, escalator, transfer terms and total payments you are actually responsible for.

When I would be very cautious about signing

These are the situations that should make you slow down:

  • The roof needs replacement soon, but the solar quote treats roof work as somebody else's problem.
  • The production estimate has no usable shade study, roof-plane layout or monthly breakdown.
  • The savings model values every generated kWh at the full retail rate even though the utility pays a lower export credit.
  • The system is larger than your usage supports, and the installer cannot show what happens to excess exports under your tariff.
  • The “discount” depends on financing, a dealer fee or a future lump-sum payment that the salesperson glosses over.
  • The quote excludes electrical or roof work that may be required once the project is inspected or opened up.
  • The salesperson uses a federal tax credit that does not apply to a new 2026 homeowner installation.
  • You may sell the home before the loan, lease or PPA terms become manageable, and nobody has explained the transfer process in writing.

None of these automatically means solar is wrong for the property. They mean the proposal has not earned your signature. Get the missing numbers and contract terms first.

Some homeowners also care about backup power, lower emissions or less exposure to future utility-rate changes. Those can be valid reasons to install solar. Just do not mix them into the financial return and then call the project “paid for.” If resilience is the goal, check whether the system actually includes backup capability. Ordinary grid-tied solar usually shuts down during an outage unless it has the right inverter, storage and transfer equipment.

Before you sign: make the installer show its work

  1. Put 12 months of kWh usage and your actual utility rate plan on the table.
  2. Get the panel layout and monthly production estimate, including shade, roof direction, tilt and system losses.
  3. Ask for monthly self-use, imports and exports. Annual “offset” is not enough.
  4. Call the utility and confirm the export credit, interconnection rules and any system-size cap.
  5. Get the cash price with every required item included. Make the installer list exclusions and likely change-order triggers.
  6. Read the full loan, lease or PPA. Check the total payments, fees, escalators, liens and home-sale terms.
  7. Have the roof assessed before panels cover it. Get any repair or replacement plan in writing.
  8. Verify every incentive with the agency or utility that administers it. Do not rely on a salesperson's spreadsheet.
  9. Save the tariff, production model, quote and financing documents. Those are the assumptions your decision rests on.

Questions homeowners should ask before buying

Can solar still make sense without the 30% federal homeowner credit?

Yes, if the installed price and utility savings make sense without it. Run the numbers using the full 2026 price, your actual export rate, the roof's condition and the contract you will sign. Do not let a salesperson put an expired credit back into the spreadsheet.

My electric bill is high. Doesn't that make solar an easy decision?

No. A high bill can include fixed fees, taxes or demand charges that panels do not erase. You need to know how many kWh you use, what each avoided kWh is worth, how much solar you will use directly and what the utility pays for the rest.

Can I use the EIA state average as my electricity rate?

Use it for broad context, not for a project quote. Your utility's tariff sets the import charge and export credit. Those can differ from the state average and from each other.

Is the PVWatts number a production guarantee?

No. It is a modeling estimate based on stated inputs and weather data, not a warranty that the installed system will deliver that output. Check the assumptions and ask the installer for a site-specific design. Keep any production guarantee separate from the modeled estimate.

Should I replace an old roof before installing panels?

There is no magic age that applies to every roof. Get the roof inspected, identify active damage and ask how much service life remains. If replacement is likely during the solar system's life, price the roof first and get any future detach-and-reset costs in writing.

Does a lease or PPA give me the homeowner tax credit?

Do not assume it does. In a lease or PPA, a third party owns the system. The tax treatment is not the same as a homeowner buying equipment. Ask the provider to explain any claimed discount in the contract and verify the tax position independently.
