Solar Panel Installation Cost in 2026: What a Home System Costs

Start with the installed cash price per DC watt. Not the price of the panels alone. Not the monthly loan payment. Not a bundled number that hides a battery, roof work and financing fees. The benchmark below gives you a reference point. Your written quote still needs to show exactly what you are buying.

2026 solar installation price per watt

EnergySage's 23rd Home Electrification Marketplace Report covers January through June 2026. It puts the median price for a solar-only system at $2.62/W before incentives. The broader median across its mix of solar system types is $2.57/W. Use the $2.62 figure when comparing a PV system without a battery. EnergySage says its marketplace data includes millions of homeowner shopping transactions, quotes, equipment selections and financing choices. That is a large dataset, but it is still marketplace data. It is not a census of every U.S. installation and it is not proof of the final invoice paid by every customer. Its consumer page also shows about $2.60/W as an average. Average and median are different statistics, so keep them separate.

Those totals are just system watts multiplied by one published median. They are not price bands and they do not predict what an installer will charge in your ZIP code. A small roof with a difficult layout can cost more per watt than a large, simple roof. Electrical upgrades, roof work, equipment and local labor can move the quote.

DOE cost model: a different kind of benchmark.

The Department of Energy's 2025Q1 model puts a representative 8 kW DC residential PV-only system at $2.95/W DC modeled market price and $2.78/W DC minimum sustainable price. The summary tables use 2024 U.S. dollars. This is a national-laboratory cost model, not a marketplace median and not a consumer invoice. Do not average it with EnergySage's $2.62/W figure. They measure different things.

Source: U.S. Department of Energy — Solar Photovoltaic System Cost Benchmarks.

What a solar quote should separate before you compare price per watt

For a fair price-per-watt comparison, divide the PV-only installed cash price by the system's DC nameplate capacity. Do not compare that result with a financed monthly payment or a package that quietly includes a battery. Make the installer split out these items in the proposal:

Quote itemWhat to recordWhy it must be separated
PV-only systemDC kW, module and inverter models, racking, installation labor, permits and interconnectionThis is the comparable base for a PV-only installed cash price per DC watt.
Battery storageBattery model, usable kWh, continuous power kW, backup equipment and installationStorage changes system scope and price; do not hide it inside a PV-only comparison.
Electrical or service workMain-panel/service upgrade, subpanel, trenching, wiring or other site workThese may be necessary for a particular home but are not the same thing as the PV array price.
Roof work and detach/resetRoof repairs, panel removal/reinstallation, attachment flashing and responsibility for leaksKeep roofing work and solar-system work visible, especially when both projects are being coordinated.
FinancingCash price, amount financed, APR, term, dealer/program fees, total payments and any required prepaymentA low payment or advertised APR can conceal a higher financed principal or payment change.
Utility and production assumptionsUtility rate plan, export credit, modeled annual kWh, equipment, roof planes and shade assumptionsSystem production and financial value depend on different assumptions; neither is established by price per watt alone.

The DOE model shows why a battery must be separated. Its 8 kW PV-only example is $2.95/W DC. The 8 kW PV system paired with a 13.5 kWh battery is $4.59/W DC. Different packages, not two quotes for the same job. Both are 2024-dollar model outputs. See the DOE benchmark tables and system definitions.

Cash price versus loan principal: check the actual amount owed

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau gives a useful example: a $30,000 cash project becomes a $39,000 loan principal after a $9,000 fee is added, before interest. That is an example from the CFPB report, not proof that every solar loan carries the same fee. On an 8 kW DC system, the math works out to $3.75/W at the cash price and $4.875/W at the loan principal. That extra $9,000 does not disappear because the monthly payment looks manageable. Ask for the cash price, amount financed, fees, APR and total payments for the exact same system. CFPB: Solar Financing.

2026 tax-credit check: the IRS states that the homeowner Residential Clean Energy Credit under Section 25D is not available for expenditures made after December 31, 2025. Do not treat an assumed future homeowner tax credit as cash already available to reduce the loan principal. For an individual project, timing and eligibility need to be checked against the current IRS Section 25D FAQs. This is the federal homeowner credit only; state, local and utility programs are separate and must be checked with the relevant authority.

Pro Tip Here is the real-world catch: a salesperson can make the payment look smaller by stretching the loan term or rolling a dealer fee into the amount financed. The system did not get cheaper. You just moved the cost somewhere less obvious. Get the cash price and loan disclosure side by side. If the salesperson refuses to show both, stop comparing monthly payments.

Questions to resolve before signing

  • Is the quoted system size DC or AC, and is the price divided by DC nameplate watts?
  • Does the written cash price include permits, interconnection, racking, installation and required electrical work?
  • Are battery, roof work, service-panel upgrades and financing fees itemized separately?
  • What production estimate, roof-plane layout, shading assumptions and utility export rate support the savings claim?
  • What happens if the utility requires additional work, the roof needs repair, or the system does not receive permission to operate on the proposed schedule?

A benchmark helps you ask better questions. It cannot tell you whether a particular quote is fair. Roof complexity, equipment, electrical work, local labor, utility requirements, storage and financing all affect the total. This page does not certify installers or price your home.

Check the price per watt on a written solar quote

Enter the PV-only installed cash price and the system's DC nameplate capacity. Leave out the battery, roof replacement, unrelated electrical upgrades and loan fees. The PV price should still include the panels, inverter, racking, installation labor, permits and interconnection work required for that system. If the installer will not separate those costs, the quote is not ready for a price-per-watt comparison.
