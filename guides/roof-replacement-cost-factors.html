Before comparing the totals

Do not ask three roofers for “a roof” and expect three comparable prices. Give them the same measured area, material, tear-off expectations and scope. If one bid includes new flashing and another reuses the old pieces, the totals are not comparable. Use the rows below to force the differences into the open.

3. Separate the quoted price from possible extras

An allowance is not a fixed price. An exclusion is not included just because the salesperson mentioned it during the visit. Write down only the amounts and scope the contractor has committed to in the proposal. If a major item is unclear, ask for a revised bid before signing.

This worksheet cannot predict what the crew will find under the old roof. Do not invent a decking bill before anyone has seen the deck. Get the unit rate, how the damaged area will be measured, what photo evidence will be supplied and how you approve the extra work before demolition begins.

Why three bids can differ even when the roof looks the same

One homeowner in southwest Ohio reported three tear-off bids of $11,000, $16,000 and $23,000 for a 1,600-square-foot ranch. The lowest bidder reportedly would not itemize the work. That is one homeowner's experience, not a local price survey and not proof that the high bidder was fair or the low bidder was dishonest. The point is that a total without a scope sheet tells you very little. Read the homeowner discussion.

Before you compare totals, line up these items. Some may not apply to your roof. The actual assembly, local code, climate, product instructions and site conditions decide what belongs in the job.

For every allowance, record the quantity included and the price for going over it. For hidden damage, agree on photos, measurements and approval before the crew does extra work. A “10% decking allowance” is not a promise that the final bill will stay within 10%. Find out what happens when the allowance runs out.

4. Resolve these points before signing

  • Verify contractor licensing where required, insurance and any local registration directly with the relevant authority or insurer.
  • Ask who obtains permits and schedules inspections, and confirm local requirements independently.
  • Get product names, installation details, exclusions, cleanup and warranty terms in the signed contract or an attached scope sheet.
  • Understand the deposit, payment milestones, cancellation terms and how additional work is approved and priced.
  • Ask each bidder to explain any unusually low price or missing scope item in writing.

How to use the result

The lowest total can be the most expensive option if half the job is sitting in exclusions. Get the missing scope priced, pin down the allowances and make the contractors explain any differences. Then compare the work that is actually promised. This worksheet does not diagnose the roof; the inspection and written proposal must do that.

For national cost context and published price ranges, see the roof replacement cost guide. For a broad arithmetic scenario based on measured area and rates you enter, use the roof replacement cost calculator.

Pro Tip Here is the real-world catch: the biggest surprise charges often sit in the lines nobody reads closely: extra tear-off layers, rotten decking, flashing upgrades, permit fees and disposal. A contractor may not know the deck is damaged until the roof is opened. That is normal. What is not acceptable is an open-ended “we'll bill you as needed” clause. Agree on unit prices, photo evidence and written approval before the first shingle comes off.
