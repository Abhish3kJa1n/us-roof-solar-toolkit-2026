How Solar Panels Affect Your Electric Bill

Solar savings depend on when your home uses energy, how your utility credits exports and which charges remain on your bill. This guide explains the moving parts.

Home / Guides / How Solar Panels Affect Your Electric Bill

Solar changes the timing and shape of an electric bill

A solar array produces electricity when sunlight reaches the modules. The household may use some of that power instantly and export surplus electricity to the grid. At other times, the home may still import power. The utility bill reflects the tariff and how imports, exports and fixed charges are treated—not just the annual production number.

For the installed-price side of the calculation, use the solar installation cost guide and PV-only quote-per-watt checker.

Self-consumption and exported electricity

When solar generation coincides with household demand, it can reduce electricity imported from the utility. Surplus generation may be exported, stored in a battery or curtailed depending on system design and utility rules. Export compensation may differ from the retail price paid for imported electricity. Do not assume each exported kWh offsets one retail-priced kWh.

Fixed charges and other bill components

Customer charges, minimum bills, demand charges, taxes, riders and other fees may remain after solar is installed. Some charges are based on usage; others are not. A solar proposal should show which parts of your bill the system is expected to reduce and which are likely to remain.

Time-of-use tariffs

Under a time-of-use tariff, electricity prices vary by time period. Solar generation may align with some high-price periods, but not necessarily all of them. Battery storage can change when power is used or exported, but it also adds equipment cost, conversion losses, maintenance and possible replacement expense.

Electric-bill input worksheet

Before using an annual calculator, identify which parts of your bill actually change when one fewer kWh is imported or one extra kWh is exported. The categories below are common examples; your tariff may use different names or rules.

If your tariff has tiered, seasonal or time-varying prices, ask the utility or installer for a month-by-month model rather than forcing it into one annual rate.

Once you have your actual tariff and energy-flow assumptions, use them to evaluate whether the full project cost is justified. See our solar investment decision guide for the wider payback framework.

Data to gather before estimating savings

  • 12 months of utility bills, including kWh usage and rate-plan details.
  • The current tariff and interconnection/export compensation rules from the utility.
  • A production estimate based on actual address, roof orientation, tilt, shading and local weather data.
  • The installed cash price and separate costs for storage, electrical upgrades or roof work.
  • Financing terms, incentives and tax eligibility confirmed from current official sources and qualified advisers.

Why a calculator can only provide a first pass

A simple calculator can compare scenarios but cannot recreate the utility's billing engine or a site-specific solar model unless it has accurate inputs and validated data. Use calculator outputs to ask better questions, not as a promise of savings. Ask the installer to show monthly imports, exports, bills and assumptions—not only annual offset.

Official information matters

Check the current tariff and interconnection rules with your serving utility or state utility commission. Tax rules and incentive programs can change and depend on timing and eligibility; verify them with official government sources and a qualified tax professional. This guide does not determine eligibility or provide tax advice.
