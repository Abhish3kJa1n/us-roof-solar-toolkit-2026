How Solar Panels Affect Your Electric Bill

Solar does not erase your electric bill just because the panels produce a lot of power. What matters is when the house uses that power, what the utility pays for the surplus and which charges stay on the account. That is why a system can generate 90% of a home's annual kWh and still cut the bill by much less than 90%.

Solar changes the timing and shape of an electric bill

Panels generate during daylight. If the house is using power at that moment, some solar electricity can be consumed on site and reduce grid imports. If the array produces more than the house needs, the surplus may go to the grid, into a battery or be curtailed under the system's rules. At night or during low production, the house imports power again. The utility bills according to its tariff, not the system's annual production headline.

For the installed-price side of the calculation, use the solar installation cost guide and PV-only quote-per-watt checker.

Self-consumption and exported electricity

If the dishwasher, air conditioner or other loads run while the panels are producing, the home can use that power directly. The leftover goes somewhere: export it, store it if the system has a battery, or curtail it if required. The utility may pay a lower rate for exports than it charges you for imports. One exported kWh does not automatically cancel one imported kWh at the retail rate.

Fixed charges and other bill components

Your bill may contain charges that panels cannot remove. A fixed customer charge can remain even when grid imports fall. Demand charges depend on peak power use, not simply monthly kWh. Taxes, riders and minimum bills may also survive. Make the installer identify which line items the model reduces and which it leaves alone.

Time-of-use tariffs

Time-of-use pricing means the rate changes by the hour or time block. Solar may line up with some expensive periods, but the highest-price window might start after the sun is dropping. A battery can move solar energy into a later period, but it is not free storage. Include the battery price, round-trip losses, maintenance and eventual replacement before counting the extra bill savings.

Electric-bill input worksheet

Before you enter a rate into a calculator, pull apart the bill. Find the charge that falls when you import one less kWh, the credit you receive for one extra exported kWh and the fees that remain either way. Utilities use different labels and tariff structures, so the table below is a checklist, not a universal bill template.

Bill componentQuestion to askCalculator treatment

If the rate changes by season, usage tier or hour, one annual cents-per-kWh input will flatten out important details. Ask for a month-by-month or interval-based bill model. Do not squeeze a complicated tariff into one average just because the calculator has one box for it.

Once you have your actual tariff and energy-flow assumptions, use them to evaluate whether the full project cost is justified. See our solar investment decision guide for the wider payback framework.

Data to gather before estimating savings

  • Twelve months of bills. Record the kWh, rate plan and any seasonal or time-of-use changes.
  • The current tariff and interconnection/export compensation rules from the utility.
  • A site-specific production estimate showing roof planes, orientation, tilt, shading, system losses and weather assumptions.
  • The installed cash price and separate costs for storage, electrical upgrades or roof work.
  • Financing terms, incentives and tax eligibility confirmed from current official sources and qualified advisers.

A worked example: 90% annual generation does not mean 90% bill savings

Take a house that uses 10,000 kWh a year. The proposed array produces 9,000 kWh, or 90% of that annual use. That sounds like a 90% offset. It is not a 90% bill reduction. For this example, assume the house uses 40% of the solar power as it is generated, avoids a marginal import charge of $0.20/kWh and receives $0.05/kWh for exported power. Those rates and the 40% self-use share are made-up example inputs, not market averages.

Annual energy flowArithmeticIllustrative result
Solar used directly9,000 kWh × 40%3,600 kWh
Solar exported9,000 − 3,6005,400 kWh
Grid electricity still imported10,000 − 3,6006,400 kWh
Value of avoided imports3,600 kWh × $0.20$720
Value of exports5,400 kWh × $0.05$270
Modeled annual energy value$720 + $270$990

The array produces 90% of the home's annual kWh, but the modeled energy value is only $990 before fixed charges, demand charges, taxes, minimum bills and time-of-use effects. Why? The house uses 3,600 kWh directly and gets only five cents for each of the 5,400 kWh it exports. The $0.20 import rate, $0.05 export credit and 40% self-use share are example assumptions. Your utility tariff and actual load pattern may be very different.

You cannot work out self-consumption from annual kWh alone. You need to know when the home uses electricity and when the array produces it. An hourly load profile is better than a guessed percentage. Ask the installer for monthly or interval-level imports and exports. You can test separate import and export assumptions in the solar savings calculator, but the result is only as good as the inputs.

Why a calculator can only provide a first pass

A basic calculator cannot reproduce every utility's billing software or predict your exact load profile. Use it to test assumptions, not to treat a savings figure as guaranteed. Ask the installer for monthly production, imports, exports and estimated bills under the tariff that actually applies to your account. If the proposal only shows annual offset, it has skipped the part that determines bill savings.

Official information matters

Check the current tariff and interconnection rules with your serving utility or state utility commission. Tax rules and incentive programs can change and depend on timing and eligibility; verify them with official government sources and a qualified tax professional. This guide does not determine eligibility or provide tax advice.

Pro Tip Here is the real-world catch: some sales proposals call the system “100% offset” because annual solar production matches annual household consumption. That does not mean the utility bill disappears. The house can export cheap midday power and buy expensive evening power, while fixed charges remain. Ask for the bill model month by month and check the export credit yourself with the utility.
